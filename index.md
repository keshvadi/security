# Computer Network Security

Welcome to the _Computer Network Security_ webbook.

This book explores the principles, techniques, and practices used to protect computer systems, networks, and data in the presence of intelligent adversaries. It covers the foundations of security, the cryptographic tools that enable secure communication, their application to network and web systems, and selected emerging topics that are reshaping the field.

The material is structured across five units:

1. [Unit One](01_introduction/) establishes the core foundations and threat models of computer security.
2. [Unit Two](02_cryptography/) builds the cryptographic primitives and public-key infrastructure necessary for secure communication.
3. [Unit Three](03_network/) applies these concepts to practical domains, focusing on network security.
4. [Unit Four](04_web/) applies these concepts to practical domains, focusing on web security.
5. [Unit Five](05_other-topics/) explores selected topics that fall outside the earlier units but remain essential, including quantum cryptography, AI agents, and cloud security, alongside the critical privacy, legal, and ethical responsibilities that must accompany modern security research and monitoring.

The book is written for undergraduate students in computer science and software engineering. It assumes that readers may have limited prior background in computer networking. For each topic, the minimum networking concepts needed to understand the security material are introduced directly in context, so that the book remains self-contained.

### Acknowledgments

This webbook is an evolution of lecture notes developed for teaching computer network security and software security courses. 

Its core structure and logical sequence closely follow the “Network Systems Security” course taught by [Prof. Joel Reardon](https://cspages.ucalgary.ca/~joel.reardon/) at the University of Calgary, to whom primary credit for the architecture belongs. Deep coverage of individual topics was made possible by Paul C. van Oorschot’s textbook, *[Computer Security and the Internet: Tools and Jewels from Malware to Bitcoin](https://people.scs.carleton.ca/~paulv/toolsjewels.html)*. The interactive webbook format was inspired by [David Wagner](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~daw/)’s Computer Security course at UC Berkeley, and the hands-on approach draws on the capture-the-flag methodologies of the [pwn.college](https://pwn.college/) team.

Beyond these primary sources, the course structure, concepts, and presentation were shaped by research in network security measurement and by observing how other groups teach the subject. Many ideas were directly influenced by or adapted from instructors across various institutions, including [J. Alex Halderman](https://jhalderm.com/) at the University of Michigan; [Stephen Checkoway](https://checkoway.net/) at Oberlin College; [Dan Boneh](https://crypto.stanford.edu/~dabo/), [John Mitchell](https://theory.stanford.edu/people/jcm/), and [Zakir Durumeric](https://zakird.com) at Stanford University; [Hovav Shacham](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/directory/hovav-shacham) at UT Austin; [Stefan Savage](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~savage/), [Deian Stefan](http://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~dstefan/), and [Nadia Heninger](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~nadiah) at UC San Diego; and [Michael Bailey](https://mdbailey.ece.illinois.edu/) and [Kirill Levchenko](https://klevchen.ece.illinois.edu/) at UIUC.
